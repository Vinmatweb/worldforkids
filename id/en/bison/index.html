Life on the prairie

Follow Bara through twelve illustrated moments, from her first days on the prairie to a new generation. The images are the current story illustrations; the approved print activity at this stage is the basic coloring page in levels 1–5.

Born on the Prairie

A female bison calf named Bara is born on the prairie in spring. Her rusty coat stands out among the darker adults.

A cow usually gives birth to one calf. During its first weeks, the calf stays close to its mother as it begins to explore the herd and the grassland.

Look closely: how is the calf’s coat different from the adults’ coats?

First Steps and Milk

Bara soon stands up and finds her mother’s milk. Her mother stays close while Bara learns to move confidently.

After a gestation of about nine and a half months, a cow usually gives birth to one calf. A newborn begins standing and nursing soon after birth.

Look closely: A calf learns quickly because it needs to keep up with the herd.

Joining the Herd

Bara stays close to her mother as they move with the herd across the grassland. Other cows and calves travel nearby.

Bison herds change with the seasons and conditions on the prairie. Moving together helps the animals find food and water.

Look closely: Can you spot which bison are walking close to Bara?

Play Under Watch

Bara begins playing with another calf. Their mothers graze nearby and keep an eye on the surroundings.

Calves practise moving and responding to one another as they run and play. Adult females remain close by while the young explore.

Look closely: which adults can keep the calves in view?

Grasses and Prairie Plants

Bara nibbles young plants beside her mother. Bison are herbivores, and grasses and sedges make up much of their diet.

Depending on the habitat, bison also eat other prairie plants. Their grazing and movement help shape grassland communities.

Look closely: Sedges look a little like grasses, but they belong to a different plant family.

Bara’s Coat Darkens

As Bara grows, her rusty coat gradually darkens and begins to resemble the coats of adult bison.

Calves are born with a noticeably lighter, reddish coat. Their colour changes over the first months. Adults have especially thick hair over the head and front of the body.

Look closely: which parts of an adult bison have the thickest coat?

Wolves on the Horizon

One day, Bara spots a wolf at the edge of the prairie. Her mother and the other adults gather together and face it.

Wolves may prey on calves. Adult bison are large and powerful; together, they can defend themselves and protect the young.

Look closely: the adults keep the calves near the middle of the group.

The Winter Prairie

Winter arrives, and Bara travels with the herd across the snowy land. Adult bison sweep snow aside with their heads to reach plants.

A thick winter coat helps bison cope with cold. Herds move according to food, water, and conditions across the landscape.

Look closely: Can you follow the tracks left by the herd in the snow?

Bara Grows Up

Several years later, Bara is an adult female bison. She remains part of the herd and continues to travel across the prairie.

Females can begin reproducing during their first few years of life. They usually have one calf at a time, after a gestation of about nine and a half months.

Look closely: What has changed since Bara was a small rusty calf?

A Look Back: People and Bison

This chapter looks back in time. Many Indigenous Nations of the Great Plains have longstanding and important relationships with bison.

Bison provided food and materials and remain culturally significant for many Indigenous Nations. Traditions differ among Nations, so it is important to speak about them specifically and respectfully.

Look closely: Bison restoration today often includes partnerships with Indigenous Nations.

A Look Back: Railways and Declining Herds

In the 19th century, railways brought commercial hunters onto the plains and made it easier to transport hides and meat. Commercial hunting and government policies brought bison close to extinction.

The rapid loss of bison also had profound effects on Indigenous Nations whose lives and cultures had been connected with the herds for generations. Conservation also means understanding this history.

Look closely: How can changes in land use affect an entire herd?

A New Generation on the Prairie

Today, Bara lives with her calf on protected prairie. National parks, conservationists, and Indigenous Nations are working together to restore bison.

Protection works best when herds have space to move and grassland ecosystems are cared for. Bison are part of North America’s natural and cultural heritage.

Look closely: Protecting bison also helps the prairies and the many other species that live there.
