World Lemur Day

World Lemur Day takes place on the last Friday of October. The Lemur Conservation Network encourages people to celebrate lemurs and Madagascar throughout October. The first World Lemur Festival was held in Antananarivo in 2014 and organized by GERP Madagascar. This is a conservation-awareness day, not a United Nations observance.

Meet the ring-tailed lemur

The ring-tailed lemur, Lemur catta, is a social primate found only on Madagascar. Its black-and-white ringed tail is a clear visual signal, while its flexible diet and ability to move on the ground and in trees help it use several kinds of habitat.

A life among the troop

Follow thirteen illustrated scenes and discover how ring-tailed lemurs grow, feed and communicate.

A mother’s first days

Ring-tailed lemurs in the wild usually breed once a year. After a gestation of about four and a half months, infants are most often born in August or September, near the end of the dry season. A single infant is most common, although twins also occur, especially when food is plentiful.

A newborn clings tightly to its mother’s fur and spends much of its early life pressed against her belly. She keeps it warm, nurses it and carries it as the group moves. Her care provides food and protection in a landscape where predators are part of the natural environment.

Carried by its mother

During the first weeks, an infant stays close to its mother. As it grows stronger, the way it rides changes: at first it clings underneath her, then it begins to ride on her back. Riding on the back is common from about three weeks of age.

The mother carries the infant between places where the troop rests and feeds. Young lemurs begin tasting solid food early, but they still return to their mother to nurse and sleep. Independence develops gradually over several months rather than all at once.

Look closely: Picture the mother moving through branches: how would her balance change when the infant rides on her back?

Following the troop

When a young lemur begins taking independent steps, it first stays within sight of its mother. It watches how the troop travels among trees, shrubs and open ground. These short trips help it learn where shelter and safe routes can be found.

Ring-tailed lemur troops include females, males and young animals, and group size varies with local conditions. Calls and raised, striped tails help individuals stay in contact as they move. A young lemur can use these visible and audible cues to keep track of the others when the troop spreads out.

Look closely: Can you spot a raised tail? It can act as a visible signal among the vegetation.

Finding food

Ring-tailed lemurs are omnivores, but plant foods make up most of their diet. They eat fruit, leaves, flowers, herbs, bark and sap; they may also take insects or other small animals. What they eat changes with the season and with the plants available in each habitat.

In some areas, tamarind fruit—known locally as kily—is an important favorite. Lemurs search among trees and shrubs and also spend part of the day on the ground. A varied diet helps them make use of a landscape where food is not available everywhere at the same time.

From branches to the ground

Ring-tailed lemurs spend more time on the ground than any other lemur species. Some sources estimate roughly a third of their time, although the amount varies with habitat, weather and food availability. On the ground, they can travel between patches of trees or search for fallen fruit.

Their long, striped tail helps with balance and is easy for other troop members to see. If danger approaches, a lemur may move quickly toward trees or other cover. Its life therefore combines movement in the canopy with frequent travel on the ground.

Across a rocky clearing

Ring-tailed lemurs use a varied mosaic of habitats: dry and gallery forests, spiny bush, scrub and rocky outcrops. Some populations also occur at higher elevations. They do not live only in dense tropical rainforest.

Rocky clearings may connect patches of trees and shrubs, but open ground also brings risks. Lemurs need to watch their surroundings and stay within reach of shelter. A healthy landscape includes connected areas of natural habitat, allowing troops to move safely between places.

Grooming one another

Mutual grooming is known as social grooming. Lemurs remove dirt and parasites, but the behavior also has an important social role: calm contact helps maintain relationships among members of the troop.

Ring-tailed lemurs have a special row of lower teeth that works like a comb. They use it to groom themselves and others, especially where their hands cannot reach easily. Grooming is therefore more than hygiene—it is one way lemurs spend time together and reinforce social bonds.

Look closely: Which two animals are grooming? What do their relaxed postures tell you?

How lemurs rest

Ring-tailed lemurs rest on branches or in sheltered places among trees. Troop members often lie close together. Resting near one another can maintain social bonds while conserving energy between bouts of feeding and travel.

On a cool morning, lemurs may sit facing the sun and expose their bellies to its warmth. Their activity can shift with time of day and local conditions, though they are most often seen during daylight. Safe resting places matter especially for young animals, which continue returning to their mothers between activity and sleep.

Look closely: Look at how the animals are arranged. Which places among the branches seem safest?

A fossa among the rocks

The fossa is Madagascar’s largest native carnivorous mammal and may hunt lemurs. Ring-tailed lemurs also face natural predators such as raptors and the Madagascar ground boa. They watch their surroundings and use trees, rocks and warning calls to reduce risk.

Predators are not the only threat to the species. Habitat loss and fragmentation, hunting and capture for the illegal pet trade create much greater pressure on many populations. Predation is a normal part of a food web; conservation focuses on protecting habitat and reducing harmful human impacts.

Look closely: Can you find the fossa? What cover is available to the lemurs?

When the forest is broken up

When continuous forest is reduced to isolated patches, lemur troops may become separated from one another. Clearing, burning, expanding farmland and pasture, and cutting trees for charcoal all contribute to habitat loss. A wide open gap can be difficult or dangerous for lemurs to cross.

IUCN identifies habitat decline and fragmentation among the major threats to ring-tailed lemurs. Small, isolated populations have fewer opportunities to move and may lose genetic diversity. Conservation therefore includes protecting remaining forest and keeping suitable parts of the landscape connected, alongside working with people who live there.

Look closely: Compare the connected forest with the places where only separate patches remain.

A morning with the troop

Ring-tailed lemurs move through an area used for feeding, resting and shelter. This is called a home range. It is not the same as the species’ full range: the full range covers all places where ring-tailed lemurs occur across southern and south-western Madagascar.

Calls and visual cues help individuals stay in contact. Raised striped tails can be seen through vegetation as the troop moves. Animals pause to feed and then continue together. When a road or cleared area divides their habitat, familiar routes may become harder to use.

Look closely: Follow the striped tails. Where is the troop close together, and where are its members spread out?

Females in the troop

Ring-tailed lemur troops have a social hierarchy in which females are usually dominant over males. Females often remain in the group where they grew up, while young adult males may move to another troop. The exact relationships depend on the individuals and the situation.

Dominance does not mean one female controls every decision. It helps explain why females may take priority at food or during encounters. Young lemurs learn the social rules by watching and gaining experience. Stable relationships are important for group life and mutual care.

Look closely: Find a female with a young animal. Which other troop members are nearby?

Scented-tail contests

Male ring-tailed lemurs have scent glands on their wrists and chest. They use secretions to mark and share scent information. During a so-called stink fight, a male draws his tail through the scent and waves it toward a rival.

This striking ritual can help settle competition without an immediate direct clash. Scent marks also provide information about which animals have passed through an area. Smell is important alongside calls and gestures. Active scent marking is different from ordinary footprints, which are not scent marks by themselves.

Conservation and survival

The IUCN Red List assessment published in 2020, based on an assessment in 2018, lists the ring-tailed lemur as Endangered and describes its population trend as decreasing. Major pressures include habitat loss and fragmentation, fire and agricultural expansion, hunting, and capture for the illegal pet trade. Drought and other climate-related changes can further reduce food and suitable habitat.

Protection includes safeguarding remaining forests, connecting habitat, supporting community conservation and reducing hunting and illegal capture. CITES lists Lemur catta in Appendix I, which places strict controls on international trade. CITES trade rules and the IUCN extinction-risk category are different systems; national laws also vary.
