Smalltooth sawfish Pristis pectinata

Meet a ray with a long toothed rostrum. Explore how it lives in shallow coastal waters, what it eats, and why it needs protection.

Why is International Sawfish Day marked?

The day raises awareness of threats to sawfishes and the need to protect their coastal habitats. It is organized by the Sawfish Conservation Society and zoo partners, including the Association of Zoos and Aquariums’ Sawfish Species Survival Plan. It is a conservation awareness initiative, not a United Nations observance.

Meet the sawfish

Five living sawfish species are recognized; this page focuses on the smalltooth sawfish (Pristis pectinata). Sawfishes are cartilaginous fish and belong to the rays, not the sharks.

What is a smalltooth sawfish?

The smalltooth sawfish is a ray with a skeleton made of cartilage. Its long, flattened snout—the rostrum—has tooth-like structures along both sides. These are not ordinary teeth: they are fixed to the rostrum and do not grow back if lost.

Its mouth and gill openings are on the underside of its body, as in other rays. The sawfish uses its rostrum to sense and catch prey, but the long shape can also become tangled in fishing nets.

Where does it live?

Sawfishes as a family live in tropical and subtropical waters. Depending on the species, they use shallow coasts, estuaries, and sometimes freshwater. Young sawfish rely on shallow, vegetated areas as nursery habitats.

The smalltooth sawfish now survives mainly in southwest Florida; its presence is also confirmed in the Bahamas. Sharks prey on sawfish, and young individuals are more vulnerable. Mangroves and other complex shallows provide shelter.

Food and the rostrum

Smalltooth sawfish mainly eat fish and may also eat shrimp and crabs. Sensory pores in the rostrum detect weak electrical signals from nearby animals, helping the sawfish locate prey even when it cannot see it.

When hunting, it can sweep the rostrum from side to side to strike prey. The rostrum helps with sensing and feeding—it is not simply a “saw” in appearance.

Life cycle

Smalltooth sawfish give birth to live young. Embryos develop inside the mother and are nourished by a yolk sac; pups are born fully formed. At birth, a soft sheath covers the rostral teeth and protects the mother. It dissolves soon after birth.

NOAA reports 7–14 pups per litter and an estimated gestation of about 12 months. Females may give birth about every other year. These figures apply to smalltooth sawfish, not to every sawfish species.

Where does the smalltooth sawfish live?

The known present-day range of smalltooth sawfish is concentrated mainly off southwest Florida; its presence has also been confirmed in the Bahamas.

Protection and survival

Sawfishes are threatened by accidental capture in fishing nets and the loss of shallow coastal habitats where young fish grow. Their slow reproduction makes population recovery harder after losses.

Protection depends on the country and legal framework. These three labels describe different things:

Global assessment · IUCNCritically Endangered (CR)

Assesses the species’ extinction risk across its global range.

International trade · CITESAppendix I

Strictly regulates international trade in specimens; it is not an IUCN threat category.

U.S. legal protection · ESAEndangered

The U.S. Endangered Species Act protects the smalltooth sawfish in the United States; separate listings cover U.S. and foreign populations.

All sawfish species are also listed in Appendix I of the Convention on Migratory Species (CMS). Protection limits targeted fishing and trade, but recovery also depends on safer fishing practices and conserving mangroves, estuaries, and other shallow habitats.

How can people help?

Protect shallow habitatsHealthy mangroves and estuaries give young sawfish safe places to grow.
Reduce bycatchMore careful fishing practices lower the risk of sawfish becoming entangled in nets.
Support researchPopulation monitoring helps identify where conservation action is most needed.
