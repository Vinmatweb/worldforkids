Sawfish Pristis pectinata

A ray with a remarkable toothed rostrum and a life tied to shallow coasts, estuaries and rivers. Discover why this ancient fish needs our attention.

Why is International Sawfish Day marked?

International Sawfish Day is observed every October 17 to raise awareness about sawfishes and the dangers they face. It was created in partnership with the Sawfish Conservation Society, the Association of Zoos and Aquariums’ Sawfish Species Survival Plan (AZA SSP), and European and American zoo and aquarium associations. It is an awareness day led by conservation and zoological partners, not a day proclaimed by the United Nations.

What is a sawfish?

Sawfishes belong to the ray family Pristidae. Their skeleton is made of cartilage rather than bone. The long flattened snout, called a rostrum, has tooth-like structures along both sides. Those structures are not ordinary teeth: they are fixed in the rostrum and do not grow back if lost.

The rostrum is useful for sensing and catching prey, but it can also become caught in fishing nets. A sawfish’s mouth and gill openings are underneath its body, as in other rays. This is one of the clues that sawfish are rays despite their shark-like outline.

Where do sawfish live?

Sawfish inhabit tropical and subtropical coastal waters. Depending on the species, they use shallow bays, lagoons, mangrove-lined estuaries and river systems. Young sawfish often rely on sheltered, shallow nursery habitats; some species can move far into freshwater.

The five species have different ranges. Smalltooth sawfish are now most often recorded in Florida’s coastal waters in the United States, while other species occur across parts of the Indo-Pacific, Africa and the Americas. A single family-wide map would hide these important differences, so this page does not show a misleading range image.

Food and the saw

Smalltooth sawfish mainly eat fish and can also eat shrimp and crabs. The rostrum has sensory pores that detect weak electrical signals from nearby animals. The fish can move the saw from side to side to strike prey.

The rostrum is not used only for hunting: research also describes it as a sensory tool. Its shape, number of teeth and body size differ among species, so exact measurements should be tied to a named species rather than applied to all sawfish.

Life cycle

Sawfish give birth to live young. In smalltooth sawfish, embryos are nourished by yolk inside the mother, and newborns emerge fully formed. Their rostral teeth are covered by a soft sheath at birth, which helps protect the mother. The sheath soon wears away.

NOAA reports 7–14 pups per litter for smalltooth sawfish and estimates a pregnancy of about 12 months; reproduction details differ by species and these figures should not be generalised to the whole family.

Biology figures on this page refer specifically to smalltooth sawfish unless stated otherwise.

Protection and conservation

Sawfish are among the most threatened groups of rays. Their rostra can entangle them in nets, making accidental capture a serious risk. Coastal development, loss of mangroves and estuaries, altered river flows, dams and targeted fishing also affect populations. Slow recovery makes the loss of adults and nursery habitat especially serious.

International protection

All sawfish species are listed in CITES Appendix I, which restricts international commercial trade. Sawfish are also included in Appendix I of the Convention on Migratory Species (CMS). Exact national protections and threatened categories vary by species and region. In the United States, NOAA lists smalltooth sawfish populations and several other sawfish species as endangered under the Endangered Species Act.

How can people help?

Protect shallow nurseriesHealthy mangroves, estuaries and river connections give young sawfish places to grow.
Reduce accidental captureBetter fishing practices and quick safe release reduce harm from bycatch.
Support evidence-based workMonitoring, reporting and research help conservation teams understand where protection is most needed.

Who is behind the day?

International Sawfish Day is a collaborative awareness event. NOAA records its creation as a partnership between the Sawfish Conservation Society and European and American zoo and aquarium associations; the Sawfish Species Survival Plan of AZA is also a partner. It is not listed as an official United Nations observance.
