Why is the snow leopard remembered on 23 October?

The United Nations General Assembly proclaimed 23 October as the International Day of the Snow Leopard in 2024. The day supports international cooperation to conserve the species and the mountain ecosystems on which it depends.

Date verification: United Nations ↗

Meet the snow leopard

The snow leopard (irbis), Panthera uncia, is a large cat adapted to the high mountains of Asia. Its thick coat, broad paws and long tail help it live in cold, steep terrain.

Animal groupMammal · cat family
HabitatHigh mountains of Asia
DietWild goats and sheep
Way of lifeMostly solitary

Life in a mountain home

Twelve approved scenes from IRBIS-01 · Two Paths of the Snow Leopards accompany verified facts about the species. The captions provide educational context, not a record of one particular family.

Safe in a mountain den

In a secluded rocky den, a female gives birth to her cubs. During their first weeks, the kittens depend on their mother for warmth and milk. Litter size varies; two or three cubs are common.

First steps beside their mother

As the cubs grow stronger, they begin to leave the den and stay close to their mother. They learn to read the rugged terrain where food and safe resting places can be found.

Play is practice

Play is more than fun. Chasing and wrestling help cubs practise balance, strength and movements that will help them live among steep rocks.

Moving through mountain country

Across rocky slopes

Broad, fur-covered paws help spread weight on snow, while a long tail helps balance the body during leaps. These features help snow leopards move across steep mountain terrain.

Tracks in the snow

Snow leopards travel across large mountain territories. Tracks, scent marks and scrapes on rocks help them leave signs for other cats.

The mother stays close

A female stays with her cubs for a long time and teaches skills they need for independent life. The cubs gradually learn to rest in cover and follow her over difficult slopes.

Food and hunting

Prey on mountain pastures

Important prey includes wild sheep and goats, such as ibex and blue sheep. In some areas, snow leopards also hunt smaller mammals and birds. Healthy prey populations matter for their survival.

A quiet hunter

A snow leopard approaches prey quietly, using rocky outcrops and uneven ground for cover. It is usually solitary and relies on surprise when hunting.

A leap for food

Snow leopards can cross steep vertical terrain while hunting. Not every attempt succeeds; after a miss, the cat must conserve energy and look for another opportunity.

Independence and the next generation

A mountain home across Asia

The species lives in mountain ranges across Central and South Asia, from the Hindu Kush and Himalaya across the Tibetan Plateau to the Altai and southern Siberia. The map shows approximate areas only.

Setting out alone

Young snow leopards eventually become independent and move away to seek their own territories. The timing depends on conditions, available space and prey.

A new generation

As an adult, a female may raise cubs of her own. This illustrated series shows broad stages of life; it is not a record of one specific family or a precise timetable.

Conservation

Snow leopards need large, connected mountain habitats and enough wild prey. Major threats include poaching, declining prey, habitat loss or degradation, conflict with herders and climate change.

How can we help?

Support coexistenceWorking with herders can reduce conflict and livestock losses.
