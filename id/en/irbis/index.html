Why is the snow leopard remembered on 23 October?

The United Nations General Assembly proclaimed 23 October as the International Day of the Snow Leopard in 2024. The day supports international cooperation to conserve the species and the mountain ecosystems on which it depends.

Meet the snow leopard

The snow leopard (irbis), Panthera uncia, is a large cat adapted to the high mountains of Asia. Its thick coat, broad paws and long tail help it live in cold, steep terrain.

Animal groupMammal · cat family
HabitatHigh mountains of Asia
DietWild goats and sheep
Way of lifeMostly solitary

Life in a mountain home

Travel with us into the mountains and discover snow leopard life through twelve illustrated chapters. Each scene connects a story with verified facts about the species.

A sheltered den in the mountains

A female chooses a quiet, hidden den among the rocks to give birth. This sheltered place helps protect her cubs from disturbance and wind. During their first weeks, the kittens are very vulnerable: they stay in the den and depend on their mother for warmth, milk and care.

Cubs are born with their eyes closed, which begin to open at about a week old. At first they cannot move around by themselves. Their mother returns after hunting to feed and protect them. At this age, they cannot escape danger or find food on their own.

The den is their first safe home. A female may move her cubs to another shelter as they grow or when conditions call for it.

First outings beside their mother

As the cubs grow stronger, they spend more time outside the den. At first they stay close to their mother and return to shelter to rest. Each short outing introduces them to another rock, slope or route between the cliffs.

At around three months, cubs begin following their mother more often. They learn where it is safe to walk and where they can hide. They gain experience by watching how she moves, pauses and responds to sounds and scents.

They gradually explore the area, but they are not independent yet. Their mother provides food, and staying near her helps them manage unfamiliar terrain.

Play that builds skills

Play between siblings is an important part of growing up. Chasing and tugging help cubs practise judging strength, speed and distance. They test how to run, stop and change direction on uneven ground.

Leaping and turning build strength and coordination. Coordination means the body works together: the eyes follow a target, the paws find a firm foothold, and the back legs help push off. Play safely rehearses movements that will later help with travel and hunting.

Cubs also learn about boundaries and how a sibling responds. Not every game is hunting practice; much of it is simply discovering what their own bodies can do.

Moving through mountain country

Sure-footed on rocky slopes

Snow leopards move through steep, rocky country. Their broad paws spread their weight over a larger area, and thick fur between the toes helps protect their feet from the cold. In deeper snow, the paws work a little like snowshoes.

Strong hind legs help with powerful take-offs and crossing rough ground. Every leap still requires a good estimate of where to land. A snow leopard moves carefully and chooses its foothold according to the terrain.

The long tail helps balance the body when turning or jumping. In cold weather, the cat can curl it around itself while resting.

Tracks and scent signals

Paw prints in snow or dust show where a snow leopard has passed. A track alone does not mean the animal deliberately marked that place; it may simply be a normal route through its home range.

Active scent marking is different. A snow leopard may scrape the ground with its hind legs or spray urine on a noticeable spot, such as a rock. These scents leave information for other cats and may help them find one another during the breeding season.

Researchers use tracks and signs to learn where the animals travel. It is useful to distinguish evidence of presence from a deliberate signal: a paw print in mud and a scent message are not the same thing.

Resting close to their mother

A female stays with her cubs for many months, providing food and safety on her own. At first, the cubs rest in a den. Later they use sheltered places in the terrain where they can watch their mother while remaining hidden.

Cubs usually stay with their mother until they are about 18 to 22 months old. During this time, they learn to cross slopes, choose safe resting places and gradually watch how she finds prey. This is not a fixed timetable; the timing can vary between animals.

The long tail helps counterbalance the body in motion and can cover part of the body during rest. It is a useful extra layer in the cold, but it does not replace a safe shelter.

Food and hunting

Food from mountain pastures

Snow leopards mainly hunt wild hoofed mammals. Depending on the region, prey may include ibex, bharal (blue sheep) or argali. The diet changes with the animals living in nearby mountains and how available they are.

Smaller mammals such as marmots or hares, and sometimes birds, may also be eaten. Large prey provides a lot of food, but catching it takes strength and the right opportunity. Wild prey helps keep the whole mountain ecosystem healthy.

When natural prey is scarce, the risk of a snow leopard attacking livestock may increase. That can cause losses for herders and lead to conflict. Natural hunting itself, however, is a normal part of a predator’s life, not a cause of its endangerment.

A quiet approach

Before attacking, a snow leopard tries to get close to its prey without being seen. Rocks, ledges and changes in the ground provide cover. Moving low and quietly lowers the chance that prey will notice the cat too soon.

The spotted coat helps break up the outline of the body among shadows, rocks and patches of snow. Camouflage does not make the animal invisible: light, wind, distance and the prey’s attention all matter. The hunter must choose a moment with a good chance of success.

This kind of hunting depends on a patient approach, not a long chase. Snow leopards are usually solitary, so they generally do not rely on a group when hunting.

A leap that may not succeed

Once close enough, a snow leopard may launch a short, fast attack or leap. In mountain terrain it must judge the slope, distance and firmness of the ground. Prey may turn, jump away or disappear among the rocks.

A hunt does not always succeed. A missed attempt costs energy, so the animal may need to rest and wait for another opportunity. That is why it uses the terrain and avoids a needlessly long chase.

Independence and the next generation

Mountain country across Asia

Snow leopards live in mountain ranges across Central and South Asia. Their range reaches into twelve countries and includes areas such as the Hindu Kush, Himalaya, Tibetan Plateau, Tian Shan, Altai and southern Siberia. Conditions vary greatly across this wide region.

A species’ range is the broad area where snow leopards occur or may find suitable habitat. One animal’s home range is a much smaller part of it that the animal uses regularly.

Roads, mining and other development can divide mountain landscapes into separate patches. Connected corridors let animals move in search of prey and new space. Warming can also change snow conditions and the distribution of suitable mountain habitat.

A young snow leopard’s own path

A young snow leopard gradually moves farther from its mother and begins to look for space of its own. Separation is not always one sudden event: before becoming independent, a cub may roam farther and then return. Eventually it continues alone.

Cubs usually leave their mother at about 18 to 22 months, though timing depends on the animal and its surroundings. A young cat needs to find an area with prey, shelter and safe routes through the mountains.

Its new range may not be right next to the place where it was born. That is why open routes between mountain habitats matter. Roads and development can make it harder for young cats to find a safe place.

The next generation

As an adult, a female may raise cubs of her own. After mating, pregnancy lasts about three to three and a half months, and she then seeks a sheltered place to give birth. She raises the cubs alone; their growth depends on enough food and a safe environment.

The future of the species depends on more than the number of kittens born. Snow leopards need healthy populations of wild goats and sheep, large connected mountain landscapes and ways for people and wildlife to share the same region.

Conservation can combine research, action against poaching, secure livestock corrals and cooperation with local herders. When livestock losses are reduced and the landscape stays connected, both people and snow leopards have a better chance of sharing their mountain home.

Conservation

Snow leopards need large, connected mountain habitats and enough wild prey. Major threats include poaching, declining prey, habitat loss or degradation, conflict with herders and climate change.

When wild goats and sheep become scarce, snow leopards may attack livestock more often. This can cause financial losses for herders and sometimes lead to retaliatory killing of the cat. Poaching threatens snow leopards directly, while roads, mining and other development can damage or divide their mountain home.

Climate change may alter conditions in high mountains and shift suitable habitat and prey. Conservation therefore needs long-term landscape monitoring and connected movement routes, not only isolated protected areas.

Legal protection follows the laws of the countries where snow leopards live.

Practical conservation can connect wildlife protection with the needs of local communities. Secure corrals, better livestock care and cooperation with herders can reduce livestock losses and the risk of retaliatory killing.

Conservation programmes also support wild prey populations, action against poaching and safe connections between mountain habitats. Solutions need to fit each region and the people who live there.
